The Drone War Comes to India: How Unmanned Systems Are Rewriting the Rules

By Siddhant Kumar·8 July 2026·19 min read

A weapon that costs a few thousand dollars can now destroy a tank worth several million. A machine with no pilot can loiter for hours over a battlefield, watch, wait, and then dive into its target like a falcon. Across the wars of the last decade — in Nagorno-Karabakh, in Ukraine, over the Red Sea, along contested borders everywhere — the armed drone has quietly become the most consequential new instrument of war since the guided missile.

India sits at the centre of this transformation, and not by choice. It faces adversaries who are investing heavily in unmanned systems, a porous set of frontiers where cheap drones now ferry weapons and drugs, and a defence-industrial base that spent decades importing rather than inventing. The result is a national scramble — to build drones, to counter them, and to rethink the very shape of the force. This is the story of how the drone war arrived, and what India is doing about it.

Why the drone changed everything

For most of the twentieth century, air power was expensive and exclusive. To put ordnance on a target from the sky you needed a fast jet, a trained pilot who took years and fortunes to produce, and an air force to sustain them. The armed drone shattered that economics. Suddenly a small, slow, cheap machine could do a large part of what a jet does — see the battlefield, carry a warhead, strike a target — at a fraction of the cost and with no pilot's life at stake.[1]

The consequences ripple outward. When strike power becomes cheap, it also becomes abundant, and abundance changes tactics. A commander who owns a hundred expendable drones fights differently from one who owns three precious jets. He can afford to lose machines to learn where the enemy's air defences are. He can saturate a target with more threats than the defender has interceptors. He can keep eyes over a battlefield continuously rather than in fleeting sorties. The drone does not merely add a capability; it inverts the arithmetic of air war.

The genius of the drone is not that it flies. It is that it is cheap enough to lose.

Three broad classes now matter. There are the large, long-endurance reconnaissance and strike drones — the descendants of the American Predator — that fly high for many hours and carry precision missiles. There are the small, expendable loitering munitions, often called kamikaze or suicide drones, that combine the roles of missile and reconnaissance aircraft: they hunt, then destroy themselves on impact. And there is the emerging nightmare of the swarm — dozens or hundreds of small drones networked to act together, overwhelming defences by sheer number. India must reckon with all three.

The lessons of recent wars

India's planners have watched the recent conflicts with close attention, because each has been a laboratory for drone warfare. The 2020 war between Armenia and Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh was a shock: Azerbaijani drones, many of them relatively cheap, systematically destroyed Armenian tanks, artillery and air defences, and the footage — released as propaganda — showed the world that a well-run drone campaign could dismantle a conventional army.[2]

The war in Ukraine drove the lesson deeper and made it stranger. There, drones evolved almost weekly. Cheap commercial quadcopters were fitted with grenades; first-person-view racing drones became precision guided weapons flown by a soldier wearing goggles; both sides raced to jam each other's signals and to build drones that could resist the jamming. The war showed that drone warfare is not a fixed technology but a churning contest of adaptation, and that the side which iterates faster wins. For a country like India, the takeaway was sobering: you cannot buy your way to drone superiority once and be done. You need an ecosystem that can keep changing.

And in the maritime domain, the attacks on shipping in the Red Sea demonstrated a third lesson — that cheap drones and missiles can hold an expensive navy at risk, forcing warships to expend million-dollar interceptors against thousand-dollar threats. That cost-exchange problem, where the defender pays vastly more than the attacker for every engagement, is the central strategic puzzle of the drone age, and it applies as much to India's coasts and border posts as to anyone's.

India's threat picture

India's drone problem has two faces. The first is the high-end military threat: adversaries fielding sophisticated armed and reconnaissance drones along the northern and western borders, capable of surveilling Indian positions, spotting for artillery, and striking. Against a technologically advancing neighbour to the north and a drone-acquiring neighbour to the west, India must assume that any future border clash will be watched from above continuously and contested by unmanned strike.

The second face is subtler and, in peacetime, more constant: the small-drone menace along the frontier. In recent years, cheap drones have been used to smuggle weapons, ammunition and narcotics across the western border, and to conduct surveillance of Indian installations. The attempted drone attack on an Indian Air Force station in Jammu in 2021 — where small drones dropped explosive payloads — was a watershed, the first such incident on an Indian military target, and it announced that the drone threat was no longer hypothetical or distant.[3]

This second threat is insidious precisely because it is cheap and deniable. A commercially available drone, modified in a workshop, costs almost nothing and leaves little trace. Defending thousands of kilometres of frontier against objects that small, that slow, and that low is a genuinely hard problem — radar struggles to distinguish them from birds, and shooting them down with conventional weapons is wildly uneconomical. India's border forces have found themselves fighting an adversary that can be, quite literally, a hobbyist's machine carrying a soldier's payload.

Building drones at home

The strategic response has two halves: build India's own drones, and build the means to defeat everyone else's. On the first, the country has moved from near-total dependence on imports toward a deliberate push for indigenous design and manufacture, driven both by cost and by the hard lesson that a nation which imports its drones imports a dependency that can be switched off in a crisis.

India already operates high-end imported reconnaissance drones for long-range surveillance, and has pursued armed variants for precision strike. But the more important development is at the other end of the scale: a flowering of domestic drone companies and defence start-ups, encouraged by government policy, building everything from surveillance quadcopters to loitering munitions and logistics drones. Policy has reinforced this — import restrictions on certain categories of drones to protect the domestic industry, production-linked incentives, and a general push under the banner of self-reliance to make India a drone manufacturing hub rather than a drone customer.[4]

The indigenous effort spans the spectrum. There is long-running national work on medium- and high-altitude long-endurance drones for surveillance and strike. There are tactical drones for the army's forward formations. There is a growing catalogue of loitering munitions — the class that Ukraine and Nagorno-Karabakh proved decisive — designed and built domestically. And there is the unglamorous but vital work of drones for logistics, casualty evacuation cueing, and resupply to high-altitude posts where every helicopter sortie is costly and dangerous.

A nation that imports its drones has outsourced its own eyes. In a crisis, the supplier decides how much you get to see.

The manufacturing push matters for a reason beyond sovereignty: iteration speed. The lesson of Ukraine is that drone warfare rewards whoever can redesign, retest and re-field fastest. A domestic industrial base — many firms, competing, iterating, close to the soldiers who use the machines — is the only structure that can keep pace. Imports arrive frozen at the design of the day they were bought; a living domestic ecosystem evolves. This, more than any single aircraft, is what India is really trying to build.

The swarm and the future

The frontier of the field is the swarm — large numbers of small drones coordinating through software and networks to act as a single system, dividing targets, overwhelming defences, and adapting on the fly. The Indian Army has publicly demonstrated swarm concepts, showing formations of drones operating together in surveillance and strike roles, a signal that the country intends to be a participant in this technology rather than a victim of it.[4]

Swarms invert the defensive problem in the worst possible way. A defender might stop one drone, or ten, but a swarm is designed to present more threats than there are interceptors, at a cost per drone far below the cost per interceptor. The answer to a swarm cannot be another expensive missile fired one-for-one; it has to be something cheaper and scalable — electronic warfare that jams or spoofs many drones at once, directed-energy weapons that engage targets for the cost of electricity, or India's own swarms fighting the enemy's. The economics force the innovation.

Counter-drone: the other half of the war

Defeating drones is now a discipline in its own right, and India has invested heavily in it. The toolkit is layered. At the sensing end are radars, radio-frequency detectors and electro-optical systems tuned to spot objects that are small, slow and low — precisely the signatures conventional air defence was never built to catch. At the defeating end are two broad families: soft-kill methods that jam the drone's control link or spoof its navigation so it loses its way or its operator, and hard-kill methods that physically destroy it.[5]

India's Defence Research and Development Organisation has developed indigenous counter-drone systems that combine detection with both jamming and, increasingly, laser-based hard kill — a directed-energy approach that is especially attractive against cheap drones because each shot costs almost nothing. Laser weapons that can burn a small drone out of the sky for the price of the electricity involved are exactly the kind of favourable cost-exchange the drone age demands, and India has publicly demonstrated such systems, joining a small group of nations with fielded directed-energy counter-drone capability.

But technology alone is not the answer, because the counter-drone problem is as much about coverage and coordination as about any single gadget. Protecting a nation's worth of borders, bases and cities means integrating many sensors and effectors into a common picture, deciding in seconds whether a detected object is a bird, a toy or a threat, and doing so without shooting down everything that flies. That integration — the network, not the gun — is where the hardest work lies.

The doctrine catches up

Weapons change faster than the institutions that wield them, and part of India's drone challenge is doctrinal. Who owns the drones — the artillery, the infantry, a new specialist arm? How are they woven into the plans of a theatre command? How does a force absorb the idea that some of its most useful aircraft are expendable, that losing them is a feature and not a failure? These are cultural questions as much as technical ones, and militaries everywhere are still working through them.

India has begun to answer them by raising drone units and integrating unmanned systems into exercises, by treating loitering munitions as a normal part of the arsenal rather than an exotic novelty, and by pushing the technology down to the tactical level where a small-unit commander can call on his own eyes and his own strike. The direction is toward a force in which the drone is ubiquitous and ordinary — as much a part of the infantry section's equipment as the rifle and the radio.

The anatomy of a loitering munition

To understand why loitering munitions have proven so revolutionary, it helps to examine what they actually are, because they occupy a strange and powerful middle ground between two older categories of weapon. A traditional missile is fast and destructive but blind and committed — once launched, it flies to a pre-designated point and cannot wait, search or reconsider. A traditional reconnaissance drone can watch and search but carries no punch of its own. The loitering munition fuses the two: it is a drone that can loiter over a battlefield, searching patiently for a target, and then, when the target appears, become a missile and destroy itself upon it.[1]

This fusion changes the tempo and economics of precision strike. A commander with loitering munitions does not need to know exactly where the enemy is before he strikes; he can launch the munition into an area, let it search, and engage what it finds. He does not need to risk an expensive aircraft or a soldier to deliver the blow. And because the munitions are relatively cheap, he can use them in numbers, accepting that some will find nothing and be expended, in a way no one would accept with a manned aircraft or a costly cruise missile. It is precision strike made patient, cheap and abundant — a combination that the wars of Nagorno-Karabakh and Ukraine proved to be devastating, and that India has moved deliberately to field in its own arsenal.

The class matters especially for India because it is exactly the kind of capability that suits its strategic circumstances. Along contested mountain frontiers where fleeting targets appear and vanish, a munition that can loiter, watch and strike offers a precision and persistence that fast jets cannot match. Fielded in numbers and built at home, loitering munitions give India's forward formations their own organic, patient, precise strike power — a profound enhancement of what a small unit can do on its own initiative.

The economics that changed everything

It is worth dwelling further on the economics, because the cost revolution is the true heart of the drone story and the source of its most difficult strategic problems. For a century, air power was gated by cost: the aircraft were expensive, the pilots more so, and the whole apparatus of air forces was built around husbanding these scarce and precious assets. The drone shattered that gate. When a capable strike system costs thousands rather than tens of millions, it ceases to be a scarce asset to be protected and becomes an expendable one to be spent.[1]

This inversion cascades through every aspect of warfare. It changes tactics, because a commander who can afford to lose drones will use them to probe defences, to draw fire, to saturate targets in ways no one would risk with manned aircraft. It changes the defensive problem catastrophically, because the defender who must shoot down cheap attacking drones with expensive interceptor missiles loses the cost-exchange even when he wins the engagement — spending millions to defeat thousands, a trade no budget can sustain indefinitely. And it changes who can play, because a capability once reserved for wealthy states with real air forces is now within reach of poorer states, non-state groups, and even smugglers and terrorists with a workshop and a commercial drone.

The cost-exchange problem is, in a sense, the central strategic puzzle of the entire drone age, and it drives much of what India and every other serious military are now scrambling to build. The answer to a cheap threat cannot be an expensive defence used one-for-one; it must be something equally cheap and scalable — electronic warfare that jams many drones at once, directed-energy weapons that engage targets for the cost of electricity, and one's own cheap drones fighting the enemy's. The economics of the threat dictate the economics of the answer, and a nation that defends against thousand-dollar drones only with million-dollar missiles has already lost the arithmetic of the war.

The counter-drone laboratory

India's counter-drone effort deserves a closer look, because it has become one of the more advanced parts of the country's response and a genuine bright spot of indigenous innovation. The challenge, to restate it plainly, is uniquely hard: small drones are difficult to detect because their radar signature is tiny and easily confused with birds; they fly low and slow, beneath the coverage of systems built for fast jets; and they can be so cheap that any expensive means of shooting them down loses the economic exchange.[5]

India's Defence Research and Development Organisation developed an indigenous counter-drone system that layers several answers together: radar, radio-frequency and electro-optical sensors to detect and track the drones; soft-kill jammers to sever their control links or spoof their navigation, sending them off course or bringing them down without a shot; and, most notably, a directed-energy laser capable of physically destroying a drone by burning through it. India has publicly demonstrated such laser-based hard-kill against small aerial targets, joining a very short list of nations with fielded directed-energy counter-drone weapons — a significant technological achievement.

The laser matters for precisely the economic reason that runs through this whole story: a directed-energy weapon engages its target for the cost of the electricity it consumes, a few dollars a shot, which is exactly the favourable cost-exchange the cheap-drone threat demands. A defender who can burn drones from the sky for the price of electricity has, at last, an answer to the arithmetic problem — one that scales, that does not run out of expensive interceptors, and that turns the economics back against the attacker. India's investment here is not a gadget but a strategic response to the defining problem of the drone age, and it is among the more encouraging developments in the country's defence technology.

The border under quiet siege

Away from the drama of swarms and lasers lies the grinding, everyday reality of the small-drone threat along India's frontiers, which is in some ways the more immediate problem. In recent years, cheap commercial and modified drones have been used repeatedly along the western border to smuggle weapons, ammunition and narcotics across, and to surveil Indian installations — a persistent, low-intensity campaign conducted with hardware that costs almost nothing and can be launched from just across the line.[3]

The attempted drone attack on an Indian Air Force station in Jammu, in which small drones dropped explosive payloads on a military target, marked a turning point — the first such incident on Indian soil, and a signal that the threat had graduated from smuggling to attack. Since then, India's border forces have grappled with an adversary that can be as modest as a hobbyist's quadcopter carrying a soldier's payload, an adversary that is cheap, deniable, hard to detect and endlessly replaceable. Defending thousands of kilometres of frontier against objects that small and that numerous is a problem no single weapon solves; it demands a layered, networked defence — sensors, jammers and effectors woven together into a common picture — and a great deal of patient, unglamorous work. It is the drone war in its most constant and least visible form, and it is being fought along India's borders every week.

The swarm and the future of mass

The frontier of the field, and the challenge that most preoccupies planners, is the swarm — large numbers of small drones coordinated by software and networks to act as a single system, dividing targets, overwhelming defences, and adapting collectively in ways no individual drone could. The swarm represents the return of mass to the battlefield in a new and disturbing form: not the mass of large armies or great fleets, but the mass of hundreds of cheap, expendable, networked machines that present more threats than any defender can engage.[4]

The Indian Army has publicly demonstrated swarm concepts, showing coordinated formations of drones operating together in surveillance and strike roles — a signal that India intends to be a participant in this technology rather than a victim of it. This matters, because the swarm inverts the defensive problem in the worst possible way: it is designed from the outset to present more targets than there are interceptors, at a cost per drone far below the cost per defence. The only answers that scale against a swarm are answers that can engage many threats at once and cheaply — the electronic warfare and directed-energy weapons India is developing, and its own swarms fighting the enemy's. Mass, once thought to be a thing of the past in an age of precision, is returning to war through the swarm, and the nation that masters both swarming and counter-swarming will hold a decisive advantage.

The manufacturing ecosystem India is trying to build

The single most important insight India's planners have drawn from the recent drone wars is that drone superiority is not a thing you buy once but an ecosystem you must grow and sustain. The war in Ukraine demonstrated that drone warfare is a churning, relentless contest of adaptation, in which designs change almost weekly, countermeasures beget counter-countermeasures, and the side that can iterate — redesign, retest and re-field faster — prevails. A capability frozen at the design of the day it was purchased is a capability that will be obsolete within months; only a living industrial base that keeps evolving can keep pace.[4]

This realisation has reshaped India's entire approach. Rather than seeking to import a fixed fleet of drones, the country has pushed to build a domestic ecosystem — many firms and start-ups, competing and innovating, close to the soldiers who use the machines and able to iterate rapidly in response to battlefield lessons. Government policy has reinforced this deliberately: restrictions on importing categories of drones that India can make itself, protecting the domestic market; incentives to encourage local manufacturing; and a general push under the banner of self-reliance to make India a hub of drone production rather than a customer of it. The aim is not merely to field drones but to build the industrial capacity to keep fielding better ones, indefinitely.

The strategic logic runs deeper than economics or even iteration speed. A nation that imports its drones imports a dependency that can be switched off in a crisis, at exactly the moment the drones are most needed. A living domestic industry, by contrast, cannot be embargoed, evolves continuously, and accumulates the knowledge and skill that compound over time into genuine technological depth. This, more than any single aircraft, is what India is really trying to build — an ecosystem that can produce, adapt and sustain unmanned systems on a sovereign basis, keeping pace with a technology that refuses to stand still. It is a harder thing to build than a fleet, but it is the only foundation that lasts.

Beyond the strike: the drone as workhorse

The public imagination fixes on the armed drone — the hunter, the striker, the kamikaze munition — but some of the most valuable military applications of unmanned systems have nothing to do with attack. Drones excel at the dull, dirty and dangerous tasks that armies have always had to perform at cost and risk to human beings, and for a country with India's demanding geography, these unglamorous roles may deliver enormous practical value.[1]

Consider logistics and resupply. Along India's high-altitude frontiers, keeping forward posts supplied with fuel, ammunition, food and medical stores is a costly, dangerous and weather-dependent undertaking, often requiring helicopter sorties that risk aircraft and crews in some of the most unforgiving terrain on earth. Cargo drones offer a way to resupply remote and high-altitude positions without risking a manned helicopter or a vulnerable road convoy — carrying loads to places that are hard and dangerous to reach, on demand, at lower cost and risk. In the mountains where much of India's most demanding soldiering takes place, this logistical capability could transform how forward forces are sustained.

Drones serve equally in surveillance and reconnaissance, giving small units their own persistent eyes over difficult terrain; in casualty evacuation cueing and medical resupply, reaching wounded soldiers or dropping supplies where a manned aircraft cannot safely go; and in a host of support roles that extend the reach and reduce the risk of human forces. These applications carry none of the ethical weight of autonomous weapons and none of the technical risk of the cutting edge, yet they may deliver more real, everyday value to India's forces than any strike drone — quietly making the force more capable, more sustainable and safer for the soldiers who serve in it. The drone as workhorse, not warrior, is one of the most practically important dimensions of the whole unmanned revolution.

The stakes

The drone has done to air power what gunpowder did to the armoured knight: it has democratised a capability that was once the preserve of the rich and the few. For a country of India's size and its strategic burdens, that democratisation is both threat and opportunity. The threat is that cheap unmanned systems in an adversary's hands — or a smuggler's — can watch, strike and harass across every frontier. The opportunity is that a nation with India's engineering talent and manufacturing ambition can build these systems better and cheaper than it ever built jets, and in doing so leapfrog into the front rank of a technology that is still young.

The drone war is not coming to India. It is already here — in the smuggling runs across the western border, in the demonstrations of indigenous swarms, in the lasers now burning small aircraft from the sky, in the start-ups racing to build the next iteration before the adversary does. The question is no longer whether India will fight this war, but whether it will build the ecosystem — industrial, doctrinal, defensive — fast enough to fight it on its own terms. On the answer rests a large part of how secure the next decade will be.
